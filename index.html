Integrating Automated Agents Into Single-Engine Cockpit Operations: A Developmental Study

Brock Rouser

Background

Summary of Aircraft Evaluations


Aircraft design and development in commercial and military sectors has progressed at a rapid pace from powered flight in 1903 to modern automated and unmanned systems. Design evaluation in aviation is multidisciplinary including individual subsystem design evaluations. Traditionally subsystems can be evaluated in isolation easily such as in wind tunnel testing and CFD simulations.


However, overall aircraft characteristics which stem from the combination of various subsystems is evaluated using test pilot feedback which is often limited due to sample size. Additionally, pilot feedback is usually captured via subjective self assessment which can be prone to bias and may not capture intricacies between individual aircraft subsystems.

 

  • Rapid progress from 1903 flight → modern automated/unmanned systems.
  • Aircraft design is highly multidisciplinary; subsystems can be tested independently.
  • Traditional methods (wind tunnels, CFD) work well for isolated subsystem evaluation.
  • Integrated aircraft behavior is harder to assess due to subsystem interactions.
  • Test pilot feedback is crucial but limited by very small sample sizes.
  • Subjective pilot assessments can introduce bias and miss subtle subsystem interactions.
  • Highlights need for more objective, scalable evaluation methods.

Leveraging Sensors in Evaluation

Existing literature suggests a link between various physiological signals and operator states such as workload and situational awareness. With increasing accessability of sensor hardware and compute resources, studies suggest using time-series physiological sensors to quantify such operator states using sensors such as:

  • Eye Trackers[2]
  • Heart Rate Monitors (HRM)[3]
  • Electroencephalogram (EEG)[4]
  • Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS)[5]
  • Galvanic Skin Response[6]

Quantifying a pilot’s physiological state using such sensing modalities may provide an additional layer of data for engineers to base decisions off of[7]. Current pilot evaluation metrics rely on self assessment tools such as:

  • NASA Task Load Index (perceived workload)[8]
  • Cooper-Harper Handling Qualities Rating (aircraft handling characteristics)[9]
  • Situational Awareness Rating Technique (situational awareness)[10]

Such metrics derived from these self assessment surveys can be difficult to capture in a time-efficient manner and may be difficult to draw statistical significance from due to potentially small sample sizes[11].

As organizations such as the US Navy seek to tune aircraft to fine degrees and future AI adjacent methods potentially being able to derive aircraft dynamics, additional layers of objective data become paramount[12,13,]. This data also furthers the usefulness of simulator based validation.

  • Physiological signals can reflect operator states like workload and situational awareness.
  • Growing accessibility of sensors and compute enables time-series physiological monitoring.
  • Common sensing modalities: eye tracking, HR/HRV, EEG, fNIRS, and GSR.
  • These sensors can provide objective, continuous measures of pilot state.
  • Adds a data layer beyond traditional subjective pilot evaluations.
  • Current evaluation tools rely on self-assessed workload, handling qualities, and situational awareness.
  • Self-report metrics can be time-consuming, biased, and statistically weak with small sample sizes.
  • Objective physiological data supports fine-tuning aircraft behavior and complements simulator-based validation.
  • Increasing relevance as organizations and AI-enabled methods push toward higher-precision aircraft evaluation.

Capability Development

The rise in popularity of complicated autonomous systems has shifted focus onto the pilot in aviation safety. The interplay between automation, mental workload, and situational awareness has become a topic of scrutiny as literature suggests that increased automation can result in an overall decrease in operator performance.


With readily available sensors and advanced modeling techniques, some researchers have proposed frameworks to increase pilot awareness by adapting the amount of automation in the cockpit by using various time series data channels to quantify conative states.

 

  • Increased system autonomy has shifted safety focus toward the human operator.
  • Research shows higher automation can paradoxically reduce pilot performance.
  • Key factors: interaction between automation, mental workload, and situational awareness.
  • Modern sensors and modeling techniques enable real-time tracking of pilot cognitive/affective states.
  • Some frameworks aim to adjust cockpit automation dynamically based on these quantified states.
  • Time-series data (e.g., physiological, behavioral) supports adaptive automation strategies.
  • Transition from traditional modeling approaches to deep learning enables richer, more responsive pilot-state estimation.

Data Acquisition

Data Acquisition Challenges

Utilizing a variety of physiological sensing modalities has several practical challenges that must be overcome to facilitate analysis for time-series data.

  • Software Dependencies
    • Various sensor libraries provided by vendors require different operating systems, programming languages, and processor architecture (32 vs 64 bit)
  • Storage
    • High frequency data from various sources should be stored in a common database in a common format
    • Due to time-series nature of the data, synchronization is paramount to a high quality analysis
  • Scalability
    • Physiological data collection in an ecological setting may require different sensors for different tasks and may even need to consider multiple operators
  • Collecting physiological time-series data introduces major practical challenges.
  • Sensor software is fragmented—different vendors require different OSs, libraries, and architectures.
  • High-frequency signals from multiple sensors must be stored in a unified format to enable analysis.
  • Synchronization across data streams is critical; even small timing offsets can invalidate results for event-related analysis.
  • Scaling data collection to real-world, ecological settings increases complexity.
  • Different tasks may require different sensor sets, and multi-operator scenarios multiply data volume and coordination needs.

Current Software Architecture

To overcome these challenges, a novel data acquisition system was developed utilizing open source tools and a distributed architecture.

  • Top layer uses a single control application to take in user input for testing configuration (which sensors, participant id#, etc.)
  • Listeners receive configuration and manage sensor processes
  • Sensors utilize MQTT Broker to route data to appropriate subscribers
  • Subscribers include:
    • Common time-series database (InfluxDB)
    • Web based visualizations for real time dashboards (Grafana)
    • Real time processing applications (Statistical models)
  • This architecture was built to address the major data-acquisition challenges: fragmentation, synchronization, and scalability.
  • A single control application simplifies experiment setup—researchers can select sensors, assign participant IDs, and manage configurations from one interface.
  • Configuration is then pushed to distributed listeners, which handle launching, supervising, and shutting down sensor processes.
  • All sensors publish data through an MQTT broker, providing a lightweight and scalable routing layer for high-frequency streams.
  • Downstream subscribers include a unified InfluxDB instance for time-series storage, ensuring consistent formatting and synchronized timestamps.
  • Grafana dashboards allow real-time monitoring of sensor status and participant signals.
  • Real-time processing components can subscribe as well, enabling on-the-fly modeling, feedback loops, or workload estimation.
  • Overall, the system supports modularity, cross-platform sensor integration, and ecological scalability.

Experimental Work

Workload and Automation

First battery of tests were focused on evaluating sensor capabilities and inferential modeling of multivariate physiological datasets. Experimental procedures focused on two independent variables.

  • Working memory load via secondary task (n-back)
  • Automation state (autopilot on/off)

Data acquired included EEG, HRM, NASA-TLX, and flight metrics. The total dataset exported from the common time-series database contained ~145 million records.

Initial frequentist analysis failed when modeling physiological data due to invalid assumptions of independence and linearity. Due to this, analysis utilized general multivariate mixed effects modeling with parameters optimized via Bayesian MCMC sampling.

  • This first test series was designed to evaluate our sensors and modeling pipeline so we could reliably capture workload and automation effects.
  • We manipulated two key factors: working-memory demand via an n-back secondary task, and automation level by toggling the autopilot.
  • Collected data streams included EEG, heart-rate metrics, subjective workload via NASA-TLX, and detailed flight parameters.
  • The unified time-series export was large—about 145 million records—highlighting both the richness and complexity of the dataset.
  • Initial frequentist models broke down because physiological data violate independence and linearity assumptions.
  • As a result, we shifted to multivariate mixed-effects models, estimating parameters using Bayesian MCMC to properly handle assumptions for linear models.
  • This approach allowed us to model pilot state more realistically and leverage the full multivariate dataset.

Linear Modeling

Univariate and multivariate linear models can be optimized via ordinary least squares fitting

\[ Y= \beta X + \epsilon \]

\[ \beta = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty \]

However, this requires several assumptions which are dependant on data:

  • Normality in residuals
  • Linear parameters
  • No multicolinearity
  • Homoscedasticity (constant residual variance )

Assumptions such as these can be validated via statistical tests such as Mardia’s skewness/kurtosis, VIF analysis, Breusch-Pagan, etc. Violating these assumptions may result in a model that does not fit well.

  • Linear models are attractive because they’re simple, interpretable, and computationally cheap—OLS gives a closed-form solution for the coefficients.
  • But OLS only works well when several key assumptions about the data hold.
  • Physiological and time-series signals often violate these assumptions, especially normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity.
  • Multicollinearity is another issue—many physiological channels covary strongly, which can destabilize coefficient estimates.
  • These assumptions can be tested statistically using tools like Mardia’s tests for multivariate normality, VIF for collinearity, and Breusch-Pagan for heteroscedasticity.
  • When these assumptions break, the resulting model may look valid mathematically but won’t generalize or represent the underlying physiological dynamics.
  • This motivates moving to more flexible models later in the pipeline, such as mixed-effects or Bayesian approaches.

Bayesian Solution

Bayes’ theorem is expressed as: \[ P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)} \]

Considering our model parameters as a distribution allows for estimation via application of this theorem.

\[ P(\beta | y,X)=\frac{P(y| \beta, X) \cdot P(\beta | X)}{P(y | X)} \]

  • The core idea behind the Bayesian approach is that parameters are treated as probability distributions rather than fixed values.
  • Bayes’ theorem gives us a principled way to update our beliefs about model parameters after observing data.
  • In this context, the likelihood captures how probable the observed physiological data are given a specific set of parameters.
  • The prior encodes any assumptions or domain knowledge we have about plausible parameter values before seeing the data.
  • The posterior combines the two — this becomes our updated belief about the parameters after incorporating the data.
  • This framework handles nonlinearity, correlated predictors, and non-normal residuals much more gracefully than classical OLS.
  • The Bayesian approach is especially valuable for multivariate physiological signals, where independence assumptions break down.
  • The two code examples illustrate sampling from the posterior using MCMC, producing full distributions rather than single point estimates.
  • These posterior distributions allow us to quantify uncertainty directly—something classical methods often struggle with in noisy physiological datasets.

  • These findings support time-series analysis such as feature extraction in order to better infer what is significant across participants
  • Overall, the results highlight the limits of subjective workload measures and the value of objective physiological signals.
  • With only five participants and four conditions, the NASA-TLX showed no statistically significant differences, even when included directly in the model.
  • In contrast, physiological channels were much more sensitive to changes in workload and automation.

Spatial Disorientation

Continuing experimentation focused on the concept of spatial disorientation (SD) in aviation. This study investigated the physiological and cognitive effects of an induced SD illusion during simulated flight using multimodal sensor data.

  • Visual and vestibular illusions administered via motion decoupling
  • Data acquired included EEG, HRM, NASA-TLX, and flight metrics

A similar analysis approach was utilized after frequentist modeling checks failed. Additionally, this study focused on exploring different priors for model parameters

  • Our second case study focuses on SD which while important to aviation safety in its own right, is particulairly interesting due to its cognitive nature
  • SD was induced in a flight simulator using visual and vestibular illusions created via motion decoupling.
  • We collected multimodal physiological data: EEG, heart rate metrics, subjective workload (NASA-TLX), and detailed flight metrics.
  • Initial frequentist models failed again due to nonlinearity and dependency issues, so we applied a similar Bayesian modeling approach.
  • This study also explored the effect of different priors on parameter estimation, highlighting the flexibility of Bayesian methods for complex physiological datasets.
  • The goal was to understand how SD affects both cognitive and physiological states during flight, providing insights for adaptive training or cockpit systems.

Model Checks


A limitation of Bayesian General Linear Modeling is the potential bias introduced when using improper priors. All channels passed statistical tests for univariate normality, but failed for multivariate normality.

Two families of distributions were used as priors for HR/RR and posterior predictive checks were conducted to evaluate model fit.

  • One limitation of Bayesian GLMs is potential bias from improper priors, so careful selection is critical.
  • All physiological channels passed univariate normality tests but failed multivariate normality, highlighting dependencies between signals.
  • To address this, we tested two families of distributions as priors for HR and RR channels.
  • Posterior predictive checks were conducted to assess model fit, ensuring that the chosen priors produced realistic simulated data.
  • These checks help validate that the Bayesian model captures the joint structure of multivariate physiological signals.
  • Visualizations show how well posterior predictions align with observed data, providing confidence in model reliability.

Adaptive Automation

Adaptive vs Adaptable Automation

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has formalized levels of autonomy in vehicles. In the most extreme examples of autonomous systems such as full self driving cars the level of autonomy is decided by the driver. This is an example of adaptable automation.

Adaptive automation refers to autonomous systems in which the system itself adjusts its level of autonomy in response to certain conditions. An example of this is auto ground collision avoidance system in the F-35.

  • Automation in vehicles and aircraft can be classified as either adaptable or adaptive.
  • Adaptable automation gives the operator control over the level of autonomy. For example, in fully self-driving cars, the driver decides when the system is engaged.
  • Adaptive automation allows the system to adjust autonomy automatically based on conditions.
  • A notable aviation example is the F-35’s auto ground collision avoidance system, which dynamically intervenes to prevent unsafe flight.
  • Understanding this distinction is critical when designing systems that balance pilot workload, situational awareness, and safety.
  • The figure illustrates the spectrum of autonomy levels, helping visualize where adaptive and adaptable systems fit.

Agentic vs Advisory Systems and Levels of Autonomy

Due to the rise of agentic AI capable of taking in information, planning, and executing tasks, it may not be long until such systems are implemented in the aviation sector.

The use of physiological data may benefit the development of such systems. Leveraging ecologically valid data may both benefit developers as an additional real-time stream of pilot state as well as evaluators who need to be able to assess whether an autonomous system is actually helpful from a human factors perspective.

  • Agentic AI refers to systems that can perceive, plan, and act autonomously, and their integration into aviation is becoming increasingly plausible.
  • Advisory systems, in contrast, provide recommendations or guidance without directly controlling the aircraft.
  • Physiological data can enhance these systems by providing a real-time measure of pilot state, such as workload or situational awareness.
  • This enables adaptive interfaces that respond to pilot needs while maintaining safety and human oversight.
  • Ecologically valid physiological datasets help both developers—by improving system responsiveness—and evaluators—by assessing whether autonomy truly supports the human operator.
